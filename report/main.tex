\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Inferring causal effects from observational data is an important problem in many fields, such as medicine, finance, decision making, etc. 

It is especially a problem in healthcare and drug design, where we are often interested in determining the effect of some risk factor on some disease outcome. in many cases, due to ethical concerns, randomized controlled trials are not possible. 

Mendelian randomization (MR) has been a useful in circumventing this issue by using genetic variants, particularly single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), as instrumental variables (IVs), particularly with the recent rise in publically available GWAS summary statistics. 

Instrumental variables have the following assumptions:
* relevance
* independence
* exclusion

Mendelian randomization makes use of Mendel's law of segregation and law of independent assortment to satisfy these conditions..? maybe??

MR analysis can typically be broken down into 3 main steps. 
1. GWAS inputs for both our exposure and outcome of interest. this can come from GWAS summary statistics
2. determining which SNPs are IVs
3. estimating causal effects


Existing problems

there are 2 main issues that may violate the validity of MR.

1. pleiotropy: where one SNP affects multiple traits. in particular horizontal pleiotropy, where the SNP can  affect other exposure-outcome confounders, can pose an issue to the IV assumption of independence. there's also uncorrelated horizontal pleiotropy - which is akin to random noise and resuts in inflated variance. (snp affects outcome through pathways that are independent of the exposure)

2. GWAS sample structure. most MR methods use 2 GWAS samples, one for the exposure and one for the outcome. most 2 sample MR methods assume that these 2 samples are independent of one another, but this may not be true. due to the size and way gwas is collected, it is very likely there may be significant overlap. there is also the issue of cryptic relatedness, where distant relatives are in the GWAS data but not reported, and population stratification where there are different ancestries/ethnicities in the GWAS data that is unaccounted for.

3. linkage disequilibrium. in order to assume independence of SNPs, many methods will do LD clumping. however, complex traits may be impacted by multiple SNPs in LD, and LD clumping may resut in a loss of power and information. (we won't be able to model as much variation in the exposure).

\section{Some best practices}

Some of these are taken from
\href{http://faculty.marshall.usc.edu/Jacob-Bien/papers/manuscript-checklist.pdf}{Jacob
Bien}. Note the use of the ShorTeX itemize environment style below.

\bitem
\item Section titles should be all title case or all sentence case. Don't mix and match.
\item I prefer data set to dataset.
\item I prefer data to be singular. There remains debate on this point. When you
use the word ``datum'' in a sentence, then we can argue. Data is a mass noun,
like ``information''. We don't say ``How many data are enough?'', we say ``How much
data is enough.'' Enough said.  
\item Terminology is lower case, unless it's a person's name: \Nystrom extension
and lasso.
\item Equations are parts of the sentence. Displayed equations almost always
have a comma or period after. Very rarely is there a colon or comma \emph{before}
a displayed equation.
\item Don't start sentences with math (``$\Sigma$ is the covariance of
$\skX$.'') or the name of a software package that's lowercase, \eg
``\texttt{glmnet} is my favourite software''.
\item Don't use contractions.
\item No need to put dollar signs around numbers: 12 versus $12$.
\item DO put dollar signs around math: $p$ not p.
\item Use $x\gg y$ not $x >>y$.
\item Careful with parentheticals and references. Wrong: (see, \eg
\citet{Akaike1973}). Right: (see, \eg \citealp{Akaike1973}).
\item Never use \texttt{eqnarray}, always use \texttt{align}. Note that ShorTeX
makes \verb+\[   \]+ into an align environment, so you can just use that always.
\item For editing purposes, it is much better if the text is hard-wrapped rather
than soft wrapped.
\eitem
