\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A central goal in medicine and biomedical research is to infer causal relationships between risk factors (exposures) and outcomes, to improve our understanding of disease mechanisms and inform drug design. However, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which are the gold standard in causal inference, are often infeasible due to ethical or practical constraints, motivating the need to infer causal effects from observational data.

With the increasing availability of genome-wide association study (GWAS) data, Mendelian randomization (MR) has emerged as a valuable framework for causal inference [1]. MR uses genetic variants, typically single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), as instrumental variables (IVs) for an exposure, to estimate causal effects on an outcome [1].

MR analysis can generally be broken down into three steps [1]:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Obtaining GWAS summary statistics for the exposure and the outcome.
    \item Selecting SNPs that serve as valid IVs.
    \item Causal effect estimation of the exposure on the outcome
\end{enumerate}

For IVs to be valid, three assumptions must hold [2]:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Relevance: IVs are associated with the exposure.
    \item Independence: IVs are independent of any confounders of the exposure–outcome relationship.
    \item Exclusion restriction: IVs influence the outcome only through the exposure.
\end{enumerate}

However, in practice, these assumptions are frequently violated due to pleiotropy, sample structure, and linkage disequilibrium (LD) [1, 3].

Pleiotropy occurs when a SNP affects multiple traits. Correlated horizontal pleiotropy, where a SNP influences a confounder of the exposure–outcome pair, violates the independence assumption because the SNP is no longer independent of confounders. Alternatively, uncorrelated horizontal pleiotropy, where a SNP influences the outcome through factors independent of the exposure, violates the exclusion assumption. Uncorrelated pleiotropy acts like random noise, inflating standard errors, whereas correlated pleiotropy biases causal estimates. In contrast, vertical pleiotropy, where the SNP affects the outcome only through its effect on the exposure, is consistent with the IV assumptions and reflects the true causal pathway we are interested in estimating.


Sample structure issues include population stratification, cryptic relatedness, and sample overlap between the exposure and outcome GWAS data. Population stratification arises when allele frequencies and trait values differ systematically across ancestral subgroups in the sample (typically due to geography or ethnicity). This violates the independence assumption, as the SNPs become confounded with ancestry. Cryptic relatedness refers to the unaccounted presence of related individuals in the GWAS data. This induces correlation among genotypes, violating the independence assumption through shared environmental or familial confounders. Sample overlap occurs when the exposure and outcome GWAS data share participants. Most MR analyses use two-sample MR, where exposure and outcome summary statistics are obtained from independent GWAS samples, to avoid overfitting inherent in one-sample settings. However, if these samples overlap substantially, the SNP–exposure and SNP–outcome effect estimates become correlated. 

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) refers to the correlation between SNPs, often due to their proximity on the chromosome. A SNP that has no effect on the exposure may appear to be associated with it, if it is in LD with a causal SNP, violating the relevance assumption. Moreover, LD between SNPs affecting different traits can lead to apparent pleiotropic effects that violate the exclusion restriction assumption. To mitigate LD, many existing MR methods perform LD clumping, retaining only approximately independent SNPs [3, 4, 5]. However, this procedure may discard valid SNPs, resulting in reduced power.

Together, these challenges can lead to biased or inefficient causal estimates. Existing MR methods have made progress in addressing some of these issues, for instance MR-APSS is able to adjust for sample structure [3], while CAUSE and MR-CUE model horizontal pleiotropic effects [4, 5]. However, none of the existing MR methods are able to account for all three issues jointly. 

To address this, Zhang et al. propose MAPLE (Mendelian randomization with Adaptive determination of sample structure and PLEiotropy), a likelihood-based framework that simultaneously accounts for LD, pleiotropy, and GWAS sample structure [6].




\section{Overview of MAPLE}\label{sec:maple}

\subsection{Problem Definition}

MAPLE begins by selecting $p$ candidate SNPs associated with the exposure based on genome-wide significance. These SNPs may be in LD with one another.

From the GWAS summary statistics, we obtain $\mathbf{z_1}$ and $\mathbf{z_2}$, which are $p$-dimensional vectors containing the marginal $Z$-scores of the SNP–exposure and SNP–outcome associations, respectively.

MAPLE models these statistics as:

\[\label{eq:z1}
\mathbf z_1 = \sqrt{n_1-1} \boldsymbol{\Sigma_1 \beta}+\boldsymbol \epsilon_1\]
\[\label{eq:z2}
\mathbf z_2 = \sqrt{n_2-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma_2}(\boldsymbol{\beta\alpha}+\boldsymbol{ \eta_c }+ \boldsymbol{\eta_u} )+\boldsymbol\epsilon_2\]


where 

\begin{itemize}
    \item $n_1$ and $n_2$ are the GWAS sample sizes in the exposure and outcome data, respectively
    \item $\boldsymbol\Sigma_1$ and $\boldsymbol\Sigma_2$ are the $p\times p$ LD structures of the exposure and outcome GWAS data
    \item $\boldsymbol \beta$  is the SNP effect size on the exposure
    \item $\boldsymbol \alpha$ is the causal effect of the exposure on the outcome
    \item $\boldsymbol\eta_c, \boldsymbol \eta_u$ are the correlated and uncorrelated horizontal pleiotropic effects
    \item $\boldsymbol{\epsilon_1}$ and $\boldsymbol{\epsilon_2}$ are residual errors distributed $N(0, \boldsymbol{\Omega})$, where $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ accounts for sample structure
\end{itemize}

Since individual-level GWAS data are typically unavailable, $\boldsymbol\Sigma_1$ and $\boldsymbol\Sigma_2$ are often unknown. Thus MAPLE uses an LD reference panel (from a matched ancestry) to approximate $\boldsymbol{\Sigma_1} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma_2} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}$. The joint model is then:

\[
\label{eq:mn}
(\mathbf z_1, \mathbf z_2) \sim MN (\boldsymbol \Sigma(\sqrt{n_1-1}\boldsymbol\beta,\sqrt{n_2-1}(\boldsymbol{\beta\alpha}+\boldsymbol{ \eta_c }+ \boldsymbol{\eta_u} ), \boldsymbol \Sigma, \boldsymbol \Omega )
\]


with the goal of estimating $\alpha$.

\subsection{SNP Heterogeneity and Priors}

Due to LD, correlated SNPs may include variants not truly associated with the exposure. Instead of using LD clumping, MAPLE places a spike-and-slab prior on $\beta_j$:

\[ \beta_j \sim \pi_\beta N(0, \sigma^2_\beta) + (1-\pi_\beta)\delta_0\]

where $\delta_0$ denotes the Dirac function for point mass at zero. Thus, each SNP has probability $1 - \pi_\beta$ of having no effect on the exposure, and probability $\pi_\beta$ of having a normally distributed effect.


The prior for the correlated horizontal pleiotropy $\boldsymbol \eta_c$ is
\[  \eta_{c,j} \sim \pi_c \omega \beta_j + (1-\pi_c)\delta_0\] 

where $\pi_c$ is the probability that SNP $j$ exhibits correlated pleiotropy, and $\omega$ represents the effect of an unmeasured confounder on the outcome. MAPLE assumes that $\pi_c$ is small to ensure that we can distinguish SNPs whose effects on the outcome arise purely through vertical pleiotropy from those whose effects are confounded by correlated horizontal pleiotropy.

The prior for the uncorrelated horizontal pleiotropy $\boldsymbol \eta_u$
\[  \eta_{u,j} \sim \pi_1 N(0,\sigma_\eta^2) + (1-\pi_1)\delta_0\] 
where $\pi_1$ is the probability that a SNP exhibits uncorrelated pleiotropy and $\sigma_\eta^2$ captures the variance of these effects.

These models allow us to probabilistically assign each SNP into one of the following groups: (1) vertical pleiotropy, (2) vertical and correlated pleiotropy, (3) vertical and uncorrelated pleiotropy, (4) uncorrelated pleiotropy, (5) vertical, correlated, and uncorrelated pleiotropy, and (6) no pleiotropy.

\subsection{Estimation Procedure}
MAPLE runs in two stages, and requires marginal $Z$-scores of candidate SNPs, an LD reference panel, and GWAS summary statistics for the exposure and outcome. At the first stage, MAPLE estimates the sample structure matrix $\Omega$ using LD score regression (LDSC), which gives

\[ \boldsymbol{\hat\Omega} = \begin{pmatrix}
t_1 & t_{12}\\
t_{12} &t_2
\end{pmatrix} 	
\]

where $t_1$ and $t_2$ are univariate LDSC intercepts (for exposure and outcome), and $t_{12}$ is the bivariate LDSC intercept. Deviations of $(t_1, t_2, t_{12})$ from $(1, 1, 0)$ indicate population stratification, cryptic relatedness, or sample overlap.

The next stage involves deriving the likelihood of $\alpha$. MAPLE substitutes $\boldsymbol{\hat\Omega}$ into \cref{eq:mn} and performs Gibbs sampling to output posteriors draws of $\alpha$.

Thus in order to run MAPLE, as inputs it takes the marginal z scores of the candidate SNPs, an LD reference panel, and GWAS summary statistics. MAPLE outputs $\hat \alpha$, and posterior mean of the other estimated parameters.


\section{Main Contributions}\label{sec:sig}

In their paper, Zhang et al propose a maximum-likelihood framework for the joint distribution of exposure and outcome SNP associations [6]. The main contributions include (1) self-adaptive sample structure estimation, (2) flexible pleiotropy modeling, (3) allowing the use of correlated SNPs.

Unlike many existing two-sample MR approaches, MAPLE does not assume independence between exposure and outcome GWAS samples. Instead, it explicitly models the correlation between their summary statistics through a covariance matrix $\boldsymbol\Omega$ which captures sample overlap, population stratification, and cryptic relatedness. This matrix is estimated directly from the data using LDSC, providing a self-adaptive correction for sample structure. As a result, MAPLE can produce unbiased causal effect estimates even when the GWAS samples are partially overlapping or exhibit shared confounding,conditions under which traditional MR methods (e.g., IVW, MR-Egger) often break down.

Second, MAPLE introduces a flexible pleiotropy modeling framework that distinguishes between multiple sources of pleiotropy. In particular, the model decomposes the SNP–outcome effects into three components: (1) vertical pleiotropy, parameterized by $\alpha$, (2) correlated pleiotropy effects $\boldsymbol \eta_c$, and (3) uncorrelated pleiotropy effects $\boldsymbol \eta_u$. By modeling these effects jointly within a hierarchical framework, MAPLE captures the heterogeneity of SNP-level mechanisms, and each SNP is probabilistically assigned to one of six pleiotropy categories. 
%This flexible structure allows MAPLE include SNPs that exhibit weak or heterogeneous pleiotropy, providing more robust causal effect estimation compared to single-direction pleiotropy corrections (e.g., MR-Egger, CAUSE).

Additionally, MAPLE is designed to incorporate correlated SNPs directly through the linkage disequilibrium (LD) covariance matrix $\Sigma$. Rather than relying on LD clumping to enforce independence across SNPs, MAPLE integrates LD information into its joint likelihood, thereby accounting for correlations among SNPs when estimating effect sizes. This approach allows for the inclusion of a much larger set of SNPs in the analysis, preserving information that is typically discarded by clumping-based MR methods, increasing statistical power.

Through simulations Zhang et al were able to show that MAPLE was able to control type 1 error, reduce false positives, and yield higher power compared to existing MR methods [6]. Together, these contributions make MAPLE one of the first MR frameworks to jointly model pleiotropy, sample structure, and LD. The method broadens the applicability of MR to broader and more realistic genomic settings where traditional assumptions rarely hold.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitation}

A key limitation lies in MAPLE’s reliance on LDSC to estimate the sample structure matrix $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$. LDSC assumes that SNP effect sizes are approximately proportional to LD scores [7]. This assumption is often violated in traits with few associated SNPs, mismatched ancestry between the GWAS and LD reference panel, or regions of extensive LD, which can lead to biased LDSC intercepts [7]. Since MAPLE uses these intercepts to correct for sample structure, bias in LDSC may distort downstream estimates $\hat{\alpha}$. MAPLE also does not provide a method for incorporating uncertainty of the LDSC estimates into the uncertainty in the causal effects.

MAPLE relies on the assumption that correlated pleiotropy is relatively sparse across SNPs. In reality, pleiotropic effects can be widespread, particularly for those mediated by shared regulatory mechanisms [7, 8]. When correlated pleiotropy is dense, the model can struggle to distinguish between causal and confounding pathways, leading to identifiability issues and inflated type I error [6]. This limitation implies that MAPLE may perform best for traits where pleiotropy is modest or where confounding arises only from a small subset of loci.

Additionally, MAPLE depends heavily on the quality of the LD matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ used to approximate linkage disequilibrium among SNPs. In practice, LD reference panels are often drawn from population-specific datasets (e.g., 1000 Genomes Europeans), which may not perfectly match the ancestry composition of the GWAS samples [6]. Mismatched reference panels can distort correlation structures, leading to inflated $p$-values, spurious associations, or loss of power. MAPLE’s performance relies on the assumption that $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ represents the underlying LD in the analyzed data, which may not hold in multi-ethnic or meta-analytic GWAS data.

\section{Novel Ideas}\label{sec:new_methods}

In this project, we will focus on quantifying and incorporating LDSC uncertainty in MAPLE. Specifically, we plan to examine how sampling variability in LDSC intercepts affects $\boldsymbol{\hat{\Omega}}$ and, consequently, MAPLE’s estimation of $\hat{\alpha}$. By simulating MAPLE under perturbed $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ values, we aim to assess its robustness to uncertainty in sample structure estimation.

\section{Methods}

In MAPLE, the LDSC intercepts used to estimate $\boldsymbol\Omega$ are treated as fixed. To incorporate uncertainty of LDSC, we simulated
\[
t_k^{(r)} \sim N(\hat{t}_k, \mathrm{SE}(t_k)^2)
\] 
where $\hat{t}_k$ and $\mathrm{SE}(t_k)$ denote the LDSC point estimate and standard error, respectively. For each $r = 1, \ldots, R$, we constructed a sampled matrix
\[
\boldsymbol{\hat\Omega}^{(r)} =
\begin{pmatrix}
t_1^{(r)} & t_{12}^{(r)} \\
t_{12}^{(r)} & t_2^{(r)}
\end{pmatrix},
\]
and ran the MAPLE model conditional on $\boldsymbol{\hat\Omega}^{(r)}$ to obtain corresponding estimates $\hat{\alpha}^{(r)}$ and their standard errors $\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha}^{(r)}$.
